Understanding Depression

Myths, facts, and ways to help someone living with major depressive disorder.

Depression is common, real, and treatable.
More than 1 in 5 U.S. adults live with a mental health condition each year. Nobody is to blame for having one, and help works.
Need support right now? Call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, open 24/7.
Section 1

Myths vs. Facts

Misinformation in movies and the media feeds stigma. Here is what research actually shows.

Mental health conditions are rare. They won't affect me or anyone I know.
More than 1 in 5 U.S. adults live with a mental health condition each year. Odds are you know someone who does. (NAMI, n.d.-b)
Depression is a sign of weakness. People could "snap out of it" if they tried harder.
Depression is a medical condition, not a character flaw. It comes from a mix of genetic, biological, environmental, and psychological factors. Willpower alone does not cure it. (NIMH, n.d.; SAMHSA, n.d.)
People with mental health conditions are violent and dangerous.
The vast majority are not violent. Only about 3–5% of violent acts can be linked to people with a serious mental illness. People with severe mental illness are more than 10 times as likely to be victims of violent crime. (SAMHSA, n.d.)
Once someone has depression, they will never get better.
Depression is one of the most treatable mental health conditions. Between 70% and 90% of people with depression eventually respond well to treatment. (American Psychiatric Association [APA], n.d.-b)
Asking someone if they are thinking about suicide will put the idea in their head.
Research shows that asking directly about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts. It may even bring relief and open the door to getting help. (Dazzi et al., 2014)
Depression is just feeling sad. Everyone gets it sometimes.
Sadness is a normal emotion that passes. Depression is different from normal mood changes: it lasts for long stretches and can affect sleep, appetite, energy, work, school, and relationships. (World Health Organization [WHO], 2026)
Why myths matter: Stigma can make people feel ashamed and less likely to seek treatment. Seeing negative media portrayals of mental illness has even been linked to more prejudice. (APA, n.d.-a)
Section 2

Facts About Major Depressive Disorder

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder. A doctor or mental health professional diagnoses it when a person has five or more symptoms for at least two weeks, and at least one symptom is a depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure. (APA, 2022; Spielman et al., 2020)

Common symptoms

  • Feeling sad, empty, or hopeless most of the day
  • Losing interest in activities you used to enjoy
  • Changes in appetite or weight
  • Sleeping too much or too little
  • Tiredness or low energy
  • Feeling worthless or guilty
  • Trouble concentrating or making decisions
  • Moving or speaking more slowly, or feeling restless
  • Thoughts of death or suicide

Section 3

Treatment & How to Help

Effective treatments include psychotherapy (such as cognitive behavioral therapy), medication (such as antidepressants), or both. Many people do best with a combination. (NIMH, n.d.; WHO, 2026)

Treatment gap: Only about 61% of U.S. adults with a major depressive episode got treatment in 2021. (NIMH, 2023)

If someone you care about is struggling (NAMI, n.d.-a)

  1. Reach out. Tell them you've noticed changes and that you care.
  2. Listen without judging. You don't need to fix it. Just being present helps.
  3. Encourage professional help. Offer to help find a provider or go with them to an appointment.
  4. Ask directly about suicide if you are worried. It will not plant the idea.
  5. Stay in touch and be patient. Recovery takes time.
